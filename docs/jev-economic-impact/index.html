1. The core economic shift: judgment becomes a metered utility¶

Every prior wave of software automated procedure — if/then logic a programmer wrote down. Every prior wave of ML automated one decision at a time — a bespoke classifier per problem, requiring training data, a data-science team, and months of model risk work. Both had the same shape: high fixed cost per decision-type, near-zero marginal cost per decision. That’s why only high-volume, high-value decisions ever got automated, and why an enormous “long tail of judgment” — the millions of small calls made inside every organization every day — stayed with humans or crude rules.

Jev-like models invert the fixed cost. A new decision type is a typed question, not a training pipeline. The marginal economics:

Method Cost per judgment Latency Fixed cost to add a new decision type
US human reviewer ~$0.10–0.50 (a judgment every 30s–2min at $15–30/hr) seconds–days hiring + training
Outsourced reviewer ~$0.01–0.05 seconds–days vendor contract
Frontier LLM forced into JSON ~$0.001–0.02 3–30s prompt engineering + parsing
Bespoke ML classifier ~$0 ms $100k+ and months (data, training, MRM)
Jev-class decision model ~$0.000003 (Every’s test: 777 judgments ≈ $0.0025) 70–500ms for many questions at once writing a question

Two derived points:

  • ~4–5 orders of magnitude cheaper than human judgment, ~2–3 orders below frontier-LLM-as-classifier, ~equal marginal cost to bespoke ML but with the fixed cost collapsed to zero.
  • Because one call answers up to 255 questions in parallel, the effective unit isn’t “a decision” — it’s “a complete questionnaire about the state of the world,” priced at fractions of a cent. That has no prior equivalent. The closest historical analog is the collapse in the cost of arithmetic (human computers → CPUs), which is what made simulation, graphics, and spreadsheets economically thinkable.

This is what Altman’s “intelligence too cheap to meter” actually means in practice for this model class: judgment — not text generation — becomes the commodity input. Electricity analog: once power was metered and uniform, you stopped building a generator per factory and started plugging motors into everything. Decision models are the socket.

2. The macro backdrop (all-AI forecasts, for scale)¶

These cover AI broadly — decision models are a share of this, not the whole — but they bound the prize:

McKinsey (2023) Generative AI adds $2.6–4.4T/yr globally across 63 use cases; +15–40% on top of all prior AI impact. ~75% of value concentrated in customer operations, marketing/sales, software engineering, R&D — note that customer ops is substantially decision work. Banking alone $200–340B/yr; retail/CPG $400–660B/yr.

Anthropic Economic Index ~36% of occupations already use AI in ≥25% of their tasks; usage ~52% augmentation / ~45% automation on Claude.ai (Nov 2025), and predominantly automated on the API — i.e., business usage skews toward replacing task-steps, consumers toward collaboration.

The counterweight finding: MIT NANDA’s “GenAI Divide” (Aug 2025) — $30–40B of enterprise GenAI spend, 95% of organizations measured zero P&L return, only ~5% of pilots reached production. Crucially, the failure wasn’t model quality — it was brittle workflow integration and an investment bias toward visible top-line functions (marketing copy) over high-ROI back-office work. That is exactly the gap a decision layer addresses: generative tools failed to integrate because every judgment call in the workflow still needed a human or a fragile prompt chain.

3. The cost of AI in general — the two curves moving in opposite directions¶

The defining economic tension of 2026: the cost per unit of intelligence is collapsing while total spending on intelligence is exploding. Both are true simultaneously.

Curve down: price per unit of capability¶

  • Token prices fell ~600× from 2020–2026 (arXiv “Tiered Super-Moore” study of 318 OpenRouter + 3,237 Epoch AI models). Economy-tier half-life 1.10 years, mid-tier 1.55 years — both faster than Moore’s law. Structural break in May 2024: prices stopped being technology-driven and became competition-driven. Decomposition: ~103.7% of cost decline from software/architectural innovation, ~0% from GPU hardware.
  • Stanford HAI AI Index: GPT-3.5-class inference fell from $20.00/M tokens (Nov 2022) → $0.07/M (Oct 2024) — >280× in under two years.
  • a16z “LLMflation”: ~10×/yr at fixed capability; Epoch AI median ~50×/yr by task, up to ~900×/yr; algorithmic efficiency alone ~3×/yr. GPT-3 at $60/M (2021) → Llama-3.2-3B at $0.06/M for the same MMLU: 1,000× in 3 years.
  • Altman: cost per unit of intelligence down >10×/yr for five straight years, GPT-4→GPT-4o dropped ~150× in ~18 months; long-run claim — cost of intelligence converges toward the cost of electricity.
  • Important asymmetry: frontier prices aren’t falling — reasoning models carry a ~31.5× price premium and frontier model pricing is rising. The cliff is in capability-per-dollar, and Jev exploits exactly this: System One tasks never needed frontier reasoning prices in the first place.

Curve up: total spend¶

  • Hyperscaler capex: $416B in 2025 (up 66% from $251B in 2024) across Amazon/Google/Meta/Microsoft. 2026 guidance: $695–725B (Amazon ~$200B, Microsoft ~$190B, Alphabet $195–205B, Meta $130–145B). Goldman’s supply-side model: $765B of AI capex in 2026 alone, ~$7.6T cumulative 2026–2031. In Q2 2026 the big four’s capex absorbed ~99% of their combined operating cash flow — a ratio with no modern precedent.
  • Energy (IEA): data centers drew 415 TWh in 2024 (~1.5% of global electricity; US = 45% of it), projected to ~945–950 TWh by 2030 (~3%). AI-accelerated server consumption grows ~30%/yr and triples 2025–2030. A typical AI data center uses ~100,000 households’ worth of electricity; the largest under construction, ~20× that. Offsetting force: energy per AI task is falling >10×/yr, and decision models are on the efficient end of that — a Jev call is drastically lighter than a frontier generation.
  • Training runs for frontier models are now nine-figure events and rising; Jev’s economics hint at the counter-trend — narrow output surface means a much cheaper model to train and serve.

Why the divergence matters¶

Generative AI’s economics have a margin problem: tokens are cheap but you need a lot of them, output tokens cost more than input, and reasoning multiplies everything. A decision model’s output is ~free by construction (a distribution over ≤255 options). Jev reprices the decision to ~zero while the industry reprices reasoning upward. If the capex buildout is a bet that intelligence demand is elastic, decision models are the most elastic segment — every judgment currently done by humans, rules, or nothing is unmet demand at the new price point.

4. Five mechanisms by which this class specifically moves the economy¶

  1. The long tail of judgment gets automated. Previous ML economics required a model per decision type — so only fraud scores, credit scores, and ad rankings (the trillion-dollar decisions) got models. Jev-class models make “should this email go to legal?” economically identical to “is this transaction fraud?” — a question, not a project. The number of decision-types that clear the automation bar rises by orders of magnitude. This is the actual mechanism behind “AI touches 40% of jobs”: not whole jobs automated, but the judgment-steps inside them.

  2. Calibration = self-selecting automation. Every prior automation wave required humans to pre-sort work into “automatable” and “needs a human.” A calibrated model knows which band each instance falls in — the human/machine boundary becomes per-instance and dynamic. Klarna is the cautionary tale that proves the value: all-in generative agents handled 2.3M conversations and replaced ~800 FTEs, then quality failures forced a partial reversal — because a generative model can’t honestly say “I don’t know.” A calibrated decision layer is the missing component that makes “automate what’s certain, escalate what isn’t” enforceable rather than aspirational.

  3. The verification economy. IDC projects agentic AI at $1.3T of IT spend by 2029. Every agent fleet needs supervision, and the supervisor must be faster and cheaper than the agent or the economics don’t close. Decision models are the only plausible supervisor at scale: check every tool call, every output, every transaction, in parallel, for fractions of a cent. A large share of the agentic-AI spend forecast is really demand for trusted decision infrastructure.

  4. Decisions get embedded where they never fit before. Sub-second latency + fractions-of-a-cent cost puts judgment inside control loops (games, robotics planning layers, live systems), per-user personalization (a judgment call per user per session), and per-item processing (every listing, every message, every claim line). Each is a product category that couldn’t exist when a judgment cost a human minute or a frontier-LLM second.

  5. RLCD-style training turns usage into an asset. Calibrated-decision training on logged outcomes means every deployment generates its own improvement data — the “self-training taste” loop from the game analysis generalizes: engagement-as-reward for products, outcome-as-reward for operations. The model doesn’t just get cheaper with scale; it gets more calibrated for your specific decisions — a compounding data moat that bespoke classifiers had but general LLMs don’t offer per-customer.

5. US economy specifics¶

  • Concentration: US captures >50% of global AI spend ($336B of $632B by 2028, IDC), hosts ~45% of data-center electricity demand, and is where ~60% of jobs are AI-exposed. The gains and the dislocation are both disproportionately American.
  • Back-office import substitution: A large share of US “judgment work” is already offshored — the Philippines’ BPO sector (~1.9–2M workers, ~$40B/yr, ~8–10% of GDP, ~70% of work from North America) and India’s IT-BPM (~5.8–6M workers, ~7% of GDP, ~$205B software-services exports) are built on it. Decision models repatriate the function while eliminating the labor — value flows to US model/API providers and the firms that deploy them, not back to US workers. Expect this to show up as services-trade effects, not employment gains.
  • Productivity accounting: If Goldman/McKinsey are directionally right, decision-layer automation is the cheapest slice of the AI dividend — it requires no new content creation, no consumer behavior change, just faster/cheaper versions of decisions already being made. It’s plausibly the first place AI shows up in productivity statistics (the “productivity J-curve” resolves fastest where the task is pure judgment).
  • BLS is already pricing this in: customer service reps −5% by 2034 (−153,700 of 2.8M), claims adjusters −5%, insurance underwriters −3%, auto-damage appraisers −9.2%. Bloomberg (May 2026): 18 AI-flagged occupations covering ~10M US jobs saw a 0.2% employment drop May 2024→May 2025 while overall employment grew 0.8% — second consecutive year of divergence, led by customer service, secretaries, salespeople.

6. Products and spend categories repriced on release¶

  • LLM-as-classifier spend — a large, unglamorous share of OpenAI/Anthropic API volume is forced-JSON classification/routing/scoring. Jev attacks it at ~1/100th the cost and 25× the speed. This is the most direct cannibalization: decision models don’t compete with LLMs on generation, they drain the non-generative revenue hiding inside LLM bills.
  • Point-solution decisioning SaaS — churn-scoring tools, lead-scoring, ticket-routers, review-triage, resume screeners. When “score this” is a $0.000003 API call, a $50k/yr vertical SaaS that wraps one classifier has a problem.
  • Rules engines and RPA — the UiPath/decision-table generation of automation handled structured inputs. Jev-like models take unstructured state directly; the preprocessing + rules stack compresses into a question set.
  • Human review BPO contracts — content moderation ($10–13B market, →$26–31B by 2031), claims intake, KYC review, dispute resolution. The contract moves from “X humans reviewing Y items” to “X items auto-decided, humans on the low-confidence band” — and the band width is set by the customer, shrinking over time as calibration data accumulates.
  • Bespoke ML teams/vendors — not eliminated (regulated/high-stakes decisions still need owned models) but pushed up-market to the hardest 10% of decision problems.

7. Counterweights and risks¶

  • Jevons, not savings: cheaper judgment → vastly more judgment consumed (every item scored, every agent supervised, every user personalized) → total compute demand rises even as unit cost falls. Consistent with capex and energy curves both going up.
  • Capex fragility: ~$700B/yr of hyperscaler spending is a bet on demand materializing; MIT’s 95%-zero-return finding says enterprise value capture is lagging. Decision models are cheap enough to survive a capex correction — arguably a beneficiary, since they’re the cost-cutting tool — but the providers’ economics depend on the same buildout.
  • Calibration is per-domain and perishable. The entire economic model depends on the confidence signal being honest on your data, this quarter. Adversarial domains (fraud, moderation) actively attack it; drift silently degrades it. “Calibration maintenance” becomes a real operating cost — still far below the human alternative, but nonzero and easy to under-budget.
  • Legal accountability doesn’t commoditize. ECOA/FCRA adverse-action reasons, medical malpractice liability, employment-discrimination law — regulated decisions need reasons and a liable party. Decision models will operate inside these workflows (triage, flagging, first-pass) long before they can own them; that caps near-term capture in the highest-value decision domains.
  • One vendor, 48 hours old. Jev is API-only, waitlisted, weights unreleased, benchmarks internal. The class thesis doesn’t depend on TypeSafe succeeding — but every number above carrying “TypeSafe claims” should be re-verified before being quoted downstream.
