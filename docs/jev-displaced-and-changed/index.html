The unit of analysis is the judgment-step — the moment inside a job or a product where someone/something reads unstructured input and makes a call. Decision models don’t replace jobs; they replace judgment-steps inside jobs, inside products, and inside other software. That’s what makes the blast radius hard to see: it’s distributed across almost every white-collar occupation rather than concentrated in one industry.

1. The three waves of what gets displaced¶

Jev-like models hit three different incumbents, in this order:

Wave 1 — Human judgment labor (already underway). Anyone paid to look at unstructured input and make a typed call: moderators, triage staff, adjusters, screeners, QA. The decision model does their per-item judgment at ~1/10,000th the cost.

Wave 2 — Judgment software (the surprising one). The previous automation stack — bespoke ML classifiers, rules engines, decision tables, RPA, point-solution “X-scoring” SaaS — had its own economics: justified only for high-volume decisions. A general decision model eats the middle of this market: everything below “we can afford a dedicated model” and above “a human eyeballs it.” Your churn-score vendor, your ticket-routing ruleset, your keyword-based moderation filter are all competing with a $0.042/M-token generalist.

Wave 3 — Generative-LLM-as-classifier spend. The unglamorous majority of enterprise LLM calls aren’t generation — they’re forced-JSON judgment (classify this ticket, score this doc, route this request). Jev prices that at ~1/100th the cost at ~25× the speed, with a confidence signal LLMs can’t honestly provide. This is direct revenue cannibalization of the foundation-model APIs’ least defensible segment.

3. Products and vendors in the blast radius¶

Directly undercut: - Content moderation vendors/BPOs (Teleperformance, Concentrix, TaskUs, Accenture’s trust-and-safety practice) — the contract reprices from labor-hours to per-decision; their value migrates to handling the low-confidence band and regulatory cover. - Rules engines, decision tables, RPA (UiPath, legacy BPM suites) — they needed structured input; decision models eat unstructured state directly. RPA’s “read the screen, follow the rule” niche gets absorbed into “read the state, answer the questions.” - Point-solution scoring SaaS — lead scoring, churn prediction, ticket routing, resume screening, review triage. Each is a $X0k/yr wrapper around a question Jev answers for a micro-cent. - Moderation/guardrail models themselves — Llama Guard, OpenAI’s moderation endpoint, Hive, Spectrum Labs/ActiveFence. They were the first cheap classifiers; Jev-class models are the general version of what they do narrowly. - Keyword/regex/BERT-era classifiers inside every company — decades of in-house “is this spam/urgent/toxic” models obsoleted by a question set with better calibration and zero maintenance.

Restructured, not killed: - Fraud/risk platforms (Feedzai, Sift, Stripe Radar, Visa’s Featurespace) — their models face pressure, but their data networks, integrations, and regulatory position survive. They become decision-model customers and resellers. - Credit decisioning (FICO, Experian; challengers Zest AI, Upstart, Scienaptic; ~$12.4B AI-credit-scoring market, 15% CAGR) — Jev can’t supply adverse-action reasons, so regulated credit keeps bespoke models; but the long tail of credit decisions (micro-lending, BNPL edge cases, SME underwriting) opens up. 93% of lenders say fraud now hits credit losses and <1/3 use AI/ML fraud models — the un-automated middle is exactly the opportunity. - EHR/RCM vendors — prior-auth modules become the highest-value feature; incumbent slowness is the startup wedge. - SIEM/SOAR, ITSM (Splunk, ServiceNow) — triage layers get rebuilt around calibrated judgment; the console shifts from “queue of alerts” to “queue of low-confidence exceptions.”

Repriced business models: - Per-seat SaaS pricing — decision models don’t sit in seats. Any product priced on “number of humans doing judgment work” has its unit economics undermined. - LLM API bills — the classification/routing share of enterprise LLM spend migrates to decision-model pricing at ~1/100th. Model labs respond by building their own System One layers (or absorbing TypeSafe-style tech). - BPO labor arbitrage — the industry shifts from selling human judgment-hours to selling automation-supervision. Two decades of recorded calls/transactions become training data — the industry literally contains the data that automates it.

4. Case studies — what displacement actually looks like¶

Klarna (the over-correction). All-in generative AI: 2.3M conversations/month, ~2/3 of tickets, resolution 11→2 min, AI doing ~800 FTEs’ work, staff cut 5,000→3,800 — then a public walk-back: quality failed on complex/emotional cases, humans partially rehired. The lesson for this analysis is precise: the failure wasn’t automation, it was unbounded automation — no honest confidence signal deciding which conversations to keep. A calibrated decision layer is exactly the component whose absence turned “80% automated” into “went too far.” Klarna is the argument for RLCD, not against it.

IBM AskHR (the successful version). 94% of routine HR tasks automated; HR staff doing manual work 700→<50; 11.5M transactions/yr; $3.5B company-wide productivity improvement. Note the structure: automate the repeatable band, refine the human escalation path, iterate on thumbs-down data. That’s the confidence-band architecture implemented with 2021-era tech — decision models make it a product rather than an IBM-scale project.

Indian IT selloff (Feb 2026). ~$50B wiped off Indian IT services stocks after Anthropic’s Claude coding tooling release — the market pricing the end of labor-arbitrage IT. Decision models extend the same pressure from coding to the entire back-office judgment stack those firms sell.

Kenya moderation industry. 140+ contractors with diagnosed PTSD (late 2024) — the human cost is already a litigation and PR liability. Automation of the worst-band content is the rare displacement that’s also a welfare improvement — but it concentrates in exactly the low-income economies (Philippines, India, Kenya) that IMF flags as most exposed and least positioned to capture the upside.

5. What changes rather than disappears¶

  • Human judgment work gets harder, not eliminated. When the model eats the easy 90%, the human queue becomes 100% edge cases — harder, more stressful, more skilled. “Escalation specialist” replaces “reviewer.” Every automated judgment system needs someone who handles its low-confidence band, which is a different, more senior job — and there are fewer of them.
  • New roles: calibration auditors, question-set authors, decision-ops engineers, confidence-band policy owners. Model risk management (SR 11-7-style) generalizes from banking to every industry running automated judgment.
  • The supervisory relationship inverts. Today humans supervise software at the process level. With calibrated models, software tells you which instances need you — humans become the exception-handler for their own automation.
  • Welfare asymmetry. Moderation, claims drudgery, ticket-queues are burnout jobs; automating the easy band removes the volume and leaves the hardest material. Whether that’s better or worse for the remaining workers is genuinely undetermined.

6. What survives — and honest limits on the whole picture¶

Survives: (a) high-accountability judgment — anything where the law needs a reason and a liable party (credit denial reasons, medical decisions, employment decisions); (b) novel one-off judgment — the model is trained on distributions, genuinely unprecedented calls stay human; (c) judgment embedded in relationships — the “call” matters less than who’s making it (negotiations, therapy, diplomacy); (d) the question-set authors and band-owners — judgment about judgment is the durable layer.

Limits: - Jev is 48 hours old, API-only, unaudited — every claim above is about the class, and the class thesis survives TypeSafe failing, but the timing estimates don’t. - Calibration degrades under adversarial pressure and drift — fraud and moderation are adversarial domains; expect the confidence bands to need constant re-validation, which keeps human review alive longer there. - Regulation is the binding constraint, not capability. The highest-value judgment work (credit, medical, hiring, benefits) is precisely where liability law prevents full automation — decision models will do triage inside those workflows for years before owning outcomes. - The BLS numbers predate this model class entirely — they were already negative. Decision models likely accelerate these curves rather than change their direction.
